The problem

Boutique owners often run the business from a personal inbox. A lead from firstname.lastname@gmail.com carries no company domain, so domain-based matching and enrichment fail, and the lead can't be tied to an account or a tech stack.

How the check works

The studio is matched against an index of real, single-location Baltimore studios built from OpenStreetMap. The studio's own website supplies the domain. Its home, about, team and contact pages are searched for a person whose name fits the inbound address in any common pattern: first.last, initial plus surname, first name plus initial, or a nickname. The role written closest to that name decides how much it counts. An exact match between the inbound address and one the studio publishes counts as well.

Guardrails built in

  • Inbound addresses are checked, never generated or guessed. The test inputs on this page are format tests built from names the studios publish.
  • Only public business sources are read, robots.txt is respected, and requests identify themselves.
  • Personal contact details beyond the submitted email aren't collected or displayed.
  • SMS consent is always written as Unknown. Consent comes from the preference center, never from enrichment.
  • Every result shows its evidence, so a person can overrule it.

Known limits

  • OpenStreetMap coverage is incomplete, and some studios list only a Facebook page, which can't be read.
  • Names inside images or behind booking-app logins aren't visible.
  • Booking software loaded only after a click may not be detected.

What production would add

  • Run the same logic as a Clay waterfall on new HubSpot contacts with free-mail domains.
  • Match against the Maryland business registry, where a small studio's resident agent is often the owner.
  • Use names captured in Qualified chat transcripts as a second signal.
  • Route multi-location and nonprofit accounts to different sources, such as RocketReach and Cause IQ, since their buying committees are spread out.
